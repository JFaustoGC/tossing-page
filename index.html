01 / The question

Where you grasp
changes the toss.

Lifting a part is only the beginning.

The selected grasp also determines how the part is held during acceleration and release. A grasp that works for picking may still produce a poor throw.

With a gripper opening of just 12–24 mm, the first challenge is finding feasible grasps. The next is learning which of those grasps help the part reach its target.

02 / The method

Learn to pick.
Then learn from the toss.

Two training stages shape the grasp policy. A ballistic model and trajectory planner generate the throwing motion.

01

Geometric pretraining

Automatically label candidate grasps using gripper opening and finger clearance. Build a dataset of 1,000 simulated scenes.

02

Refine through tossing

Continue training with simulated grasp-and-toss attempts. Target hits and misses supervise the selected action.

03

Transfer to Sawyer

Keep the grasp network fixed. Combine its selected grasp with the ballistic model and a constrained trajectory planner.

The grasp policy receives no fine-tuning on real data. A global release-speed correction is calibrated on the physical robot and held fixed during evaluation.

03 / Inside the policy

From broad regions

What remains difficult

Reliable picking.
More challenging release and flight.

Of the 171 physical task failures, 145 occurred after acquisition. Most remaining failures therefore lie in completing the toss.

How to read these results

Entry, rather than retention. Physical success means entry into the assigned bin, including cases followed by a bounce out. A toss that first enters another bin is a miss.

A defined evaluation domain. The study uses eight rectilinear part shapes, one physical instance per shape, and a prescribed tossing motion.

Fixed deployment parameters. The network is trained entirely in simulation; the physical release-speed correction is calibrated once and kept fixed.
